What to Do If a Company You Use Has a Security Problem

Published 2026-11-15

TL;DR: If a company you use has a security problem, first confirm whether it affects your account or payments, then check what data you shared, secure reused passwords and linked payment methods, look for takeover signs, save evidence, and report it to the company, your bank, or the right government channel as needed.

First, confirm whether the company event actually affects you

Start by figuring out what kind of problem this is. A security incident means the company may have lost control of data or accounts. A service outage is usually a broken system, which is annoying but not automatically a data risk. A billing issue is about charges, invoices, or payment processing. A normal account problem is something like a bad login, a locked account, or a support glitch.

If the company has posted an official notice, use that as your source of truth, not random social media panic or a forwarded email that looks like it was typed by a raccoon. Look for the company’s breach notice, status page, help center, or support channel. If the notice does not clearly say what happened, ask support what data or accounts are affected and whether the issue involves security or only service. CISA and FTC guidance both point to the same first move: confirm the scope, then act on the accounts and data that were actually touched. See CISA’s incident response guidance and the FTC’s small business cybersecurity basics.

Check what information you shared with that company

Next, make a quick inventory of what you gave them. That usually includes your email address, phone number, password, account recovery email or phone, billing address, payment card details, bank account details, and any notes you entered into the account. If you ran a business through the company, add employee contact details, customer contact info, shipping data, or tax-related records if those were in the account.

The point is not to panic over every field. It is to match the company’s problem with the data you actually exposed. An email address by itself is usually lower risk than an email address plus a password, recovery phone, and saved card. If the company says the issue involved account access, treat recovery details and passwords as especially sensitive, because those are often the keys to the account. NIST’s digital identity guidance also supports protecting credentials and account recovery paths, not just the main password.

Secure the accounts and payment methods tied to that company

If you reused the same password anywhere else, change it there too. Reused passwords are the boring little trapdoor that keeps causing drama. Turn on multi-factor authentication where the service offers it, and keep the recovery method under your control. FTC guidance for small businesses is plain here: use strong unique passwords, turn on MFA, and protect account recovery options.

Also review the payment methods linked to that company. Check saved cards, bank accounts, autopay settings, and any invoice or payout details. Watch for small test charges, strange refunds, or changes you did not make. If the company handled payroll, vendor payments, or customer billing, make sure the right person at your business can still see and verify those records.

If you want a deeper checklist for locking down credentials after a password event, see what to do if your password has been leaked. It covers the account-protection steps that usually matter most.

Look for signs of account takeover or fraud

Now check whether someone has already started using the account. Practical red flags include unfamiliar logins, changed contact details, new email forwarding rules, password reset messages you did not request, locked-out sessions, or transactions you do not recognize. In business email, forwarding rules and recovery changes are especially annoying because they can quietly redirect mail while everything still looks normal on the surface.

If the company’s problem involved email, passwords, or a login portal, this is also where your breach follow-up matters most. Look for any sign that a bad actor can still get in, then act before they start treating your inbox like their own personal storage unit. If you see takeover symptoms, move quickly to contain the account and preserve evidence. For a fuller containment checklist, see what to do if you discover a data breach, since it covers the same basic next moves.

Preserve evidence and write down what happened

Save the company notice, support emails, screenshots, transaction records, and any case number you get from support. Write down the date and time you noticed the issue, what changed, who you contacted, and what they told you. If you talked to your bank or card issuer, note that too.

A clean timeline helps in three places. It helps support teams understand the problem. It helps when you dispute a charge or a transfer. It also helps if you later need to report the issue to law enforcement, a regulator, or a data-protection authority. The goal here is simple: do not rely on memory, because memory is a chaotic intern.

Report the issue to the right place

Report the issue to the company’s breach, fraud, or support team if the event looks like a security incident or account compromise. If the problem involves a card, bank account, or payment processor, contact the bank or card issuer right away and ask what dispute or fraud process they want you to use.

If the company tells you data was exposed, use whatever government reporting route applies to your situation and location. CISA’s incident response materials are a good starting point for security incidents, and the FTC gives general guidance on reporting identity theft and fraud-related problems. The exact path depends on what happened, but the rule of thumb is simple: report to the party that can actually freeze, reverse, investigate, or document the problem. If law enforcement or a regulator needs a report, your written timeline and saved evidence will make that less painful.

Worked example: a small business vendor exposes your business email and payment contact

Say a vendor emails your office saying one of their systems exposed customer contact records, including your business email and the billing contact your company uses with them.

First, confirm the notice is real by checking the vendor’s official support or security channel. Then note exactly what they say was exposed. In this case, the data sounds like contact and payment-related details, not necessarily your full financial account.

Next, check whether you reused the vendor password anywhere else and change it if you did. Turn on MFA on the vendor account if it is available. Review the recovery email and phone number on that account so nobody can reset it behind your back.

Then check your linked card or bank records for any new charge, refund, or payment change tied to that vendor. Save the notice, the support case number, screenshots, and the date you first saw the email. If the vendor account shows odd logins or a changed contact field, report that back to the vendor’s fraud or security team. If money moved where it should not have, call your bank or card issuer and start the dispute process.

What to do next if the company says your data was exposed

After the first cleanup pass, keep watching the account and any linked payment method. If you reused the same password elsewhere, update those other accounts too. If the company exposed employee contact details or a shared business inbox, check whether other employees use the same email pattern, the same recovery phone, or the same password habit. If they do, they may need the same reset-and-check treatment.

Keep an eye out for delayed fraud. Not every bad event shows up immediately, because attackers sometimes sit on data and use it later. Recheck the account after support says the issue is contained, and keep your records in case the situation turns into a dispute or a formal report.
