How to Set Up Multi-Factor Authentication for a Small Team

Published 2026-11-04

TL;DR: A small team can set up MFA by choosing one approved sign-in method, enabling it on every account that can access business email, and planning recovery access before rollout. Start with the admin account, then enroll the rest of the team, keep backup control with the business, and clean up recovery settings and forwarding rules if an account may already be compromised.

Introduction

A small team can set up multi-factor authentication, or MFA, by choosing one approved sign-in method, turning it on for every account that can access business email, and sorting out recovery and backup access before anyone gets locked out. That lines up with CISA and NIST guidance, which both treat MFA as a basic account protection step, especially for important accounts and administrative access.

What to decide before you turn it on

Before flipping the MFA switch, make a short list of the accounts that need it. Start with business email, identity or login admin accounts, shared inboxes if you use them, and any account that can reset passwords or control access for others. If an account can open the door to other accounts, it needs MFA too. CISA and the FTC both stress limiting access and protecting accounts, which is a fancy way of saying, “Don’t leave the master keys on the dashboard.”

Then decide who will administer MFA. In a small business without IT staff, that usually means one owner or office manager, plus a second trusted person for backup. Also decide what people will use to sign in, so the team is not improvising at the login screen like it is a group project.

Choose an MFA method that fits a small team

Plain-language version: pick the method people can actually use every day.

An authenticator app is often a good fit because it is straightforward and does not depend on a text message arriving on time. A hardware security key is stronger in many cases because it is designed to be phishing-resistant, which is why NIST and CISA prefer phishing-resistant methods where possible. SMS can work as a weaker fallback if a service allows it, but it is not the first choice when better options are available.

Worked example

A 5-person office might do this:

  • Make one authenticator app the primary MFA method for everyone.
  • Keep one hardware security key stored for emergency admin access, if the team can manage it.
  • Avoid offering three or four different sign-in paths unless there is a real business reason.

That keeps setup simple. Too many options sound flexible until nobody remembers which code goes where.

Set up the first admin account

Enable MFA on the account that manages email or identity settings first. NIST and CISA both emphasize protecting privileged access, and that account is the one with the biggest “please do not get stolen” sticker on it.

After MFA is on, confirm the recovery codes are saved somewhere controlled by the business, not in one employee’s personal inbox. Check the backup contact details too. Then assign a second trusted administrator so one person leaving, being unavailable, or dropping a phone into a sink does not turn into a company-wide comedy special.

Roll MFA out to the rest of the team

Keep the rollout simple:

  1. Notify staff what is changing and why.
  2. Have each person enroll their account.
  3. Verify that sign-in works before calling it done.
  4. Keep a short record of who is protected and which accounts are complete.

The FTC and CISA both push user training and access control, and this is where that advice becomes practical. People do not need a seminar. They need clear steps, a little warning, and someone available when the login page starts acting mysterious.

Check recovery and offboarding before you finish

Before you close the project, confirm that password reset methods, backup codes, and recovery email or phone details are controlled by the business. Not by a former employee, not by someone’s personal Gmail account, and not by “the phone that left with them in February.” That is part of preventing account takeover.

This is also where offboarding cleanup matters. If someone leaves, their access rights need to be removed and their recovery paths cleaned up too. The related cleanup steps are covered in What access rights should be removed when an employee leaves?.

What to do if an email account may already be compromised

If you are turning on MFA after a breach warning or a suspicious sign-in, do the cleanup first, or at least alongside the MFA setup. Check forwarding rules, recovery settings, and any other signs that someone may have changed the account behind your back. That is basic takeover containment, not paranoia.

If password leakage may be part of the problem, What to do if your password has been leaked covers the related cleanup and containment steps, including changing credentials and checking for suspicious account changes.

FAQs

Should MFA be required for every account?

Yes, for every account that can access business email or control other accounts. CISA and NIST both support MFA as an account protection measure, and the highest-value accounts should be protected first.

What if a team member loses their phone?

Use the business-controlled recovery method, such as backup codes or a second trusted administrator, and remove or replace the lost device from the account. NIST and CISA both emphasize protecting privileged access and planning recovery before there is a lockout problem.
