What should you do if you discover a data breach?

Published 2026-11-09

TL;DR: If you discover a data breach, contain it first, secure affected accounts, preserve evidence, and write down what happened. Then figure out what data may be involved, notify the right people, and restore systems only after you understand how the breach happened and have reduced the chance of a repeat.

Act immediately to contain the breach

If you discover a data breach, your first job is to stop further damage, not to investigate every detail at once. CISA and FTC guidance both point to the same basics: contain the incident, preserve evidence, and avoid making things worse while you work.

For a small business without an IT team, that usually means a few practical moves:

  • Disconnect affected devices from the network if you can do it safely.
  • Stop using any account that looks compromised.
  • Freeze changes to the affected systems so you do not overwrite useful evidence.
  • Have one person coordinate the response so everyone is not clicking around at once.

Do not wipe devices, rebuild everything, or start changing settings at random before you have a clearer picture. You want the breach contained, not muddled.

Secure the affected accounts and systems

Start with the account or system that looks compromised. Disable it if you can, or change its password right away. If you cannot disable it, sign out other sessions and force a password reset.

Then check the basics:

  • Change passwords for any related accounts that may share access.
  • Review recovery email addresses and phone numbers, and remove anything unfamiliar.
  • Look for other active sign-in sessions and end them.
  • If a shared mailbox, admin account, or device was involved, lock it down first because those are the keys to the kingdom.

Keep it simple and practical. The point is to cut off the intruder’s access path, not to redesign your whole identity system before lunch.

Check for signs of persistence or misuse

After you contain the obvious problem, look for signs that the attacker left the door open for a return visit. That includes:

  • New email forwarding or inbox rules you did not create.
  • Sign-in activity from unfamiliar locations, devices, or times.
  • Recovery settings changed to someone else’s contact details.
  • New admin access, delegated access, or role changes you did not approve.
  • Messages sent from the account that the owner did not write.

This step helps answer a key question: is the breach still active? If you find continued access or new changes after containment, assume the attacker is still in the system and keep tightening the lock.

Preserve evidence and write down what happened

Save the things that will help you explain the incident later. CISA and NIST both emphasize evidence preservation and orderly incident handling, which is a formal way of saying do not delete the thing you may need to understand the mess.

Keep copies of:

  • Logs or audit trails, if you can access them.
  • Screenshots of suspicious settings, messages, or sign-ins.
  • Emails, alerts, and error messages.
  • A short timeline of what you found, what you changed, and when you changed it.

A plain timeline is enough. Write down who noticed the issue, what looked wrong first, what accounts or devices were affected, and what containment steps were taken. That record can help leadership, counsel, cyber insurance, or investigators make sense of the incident without having to reconstruct it from memory and caffeine.

Decide who needs to be told

Once the breach is contained, figure out what kind of data may be involved. That matters because customer data, employee data, financial information, and regulated records can all trigger different notification duties.

Ask a few basic questions:

  • Did the incident involve customer names, email addresses, or account details?
  • Did employee records, payroll, or HR files get exposed?
  • Was payment data touched?
  • Does the business handle regulated records, like health, education, or other protected information?

If the answer might be yes to any of those, the breach is not just a technical problem anymore. It is also a legal and notification problem.

Notify the right outside parties

Bring in outside help early if the situation is not obviously small. That can include legal counsel, cyber insurance, law enforcement, and any affected service providers.

Legal counsel can help sort out notification duties, especially if federal or state rules may apply based on the type of data involved. CISA and the FTC both point small businesses toward planning for notification and involving the right parties when sensitive data is affected.

Cyber insurance may have reporting requirements of its own, so do not wait until the paperwork gremlins become a second incident.

If a provider or vendor account is involved, notify that provider too. They may need to secure their side, preserve logs, or help you shut down access.

Tell affected people in clear, non-technical language

If customers, employees, or other people need to be told, keep the message plain. No jargon salad.

Say:

  • What happened, in general terms.
  • What data may have been involved.
  • What the business has done so far to contain it.
  • What the person should do next, if anything.

Do not overstate certainty. If you do not know whether data was copied, say that. If you do not know exactly which records were viewed, say that too. Clear uncertainty is better than confident guesswork.

Restore safely and prevent the same breach from happening again

Bring accounts and systems back to normal only after you have controlled access, removed suspicious changes, and understand the root cause well enough to reduce repeat risk. That approach lines up with NIST incident handling and FTC best practices.

If you restore too early, you may be handing the attacker a fresh login and a polite apology. Not ideal.

Review how the breach happened

Figure out the likely entry point. Common ones for small businesses include:

  • Weak or reused passwords.
  • Phishing messages that tricked someone into signing in.
  • Exposed inbox rules or forwarding rules.
  • Too much access on an account that only needed a little.

This is also where the email question matters. If business email appears in a breach, start by checking whether someone used stolen credentials, a phishing link, or mailbox rules to keep access quietly in place. That is often how an email compromise stays sneaky long after the first sign-in.

Tighten controls that matter for small businesses

Once you know how the breach got in, fix the weak spot. Focus on the controls that give you the biggest gain for the least drama:

  • Enable multi-factor authentication on important accounts.
  • Limit admin access to only the people who truly need it.
  • Update recovery settings so attackers cannot reset access through stale contact info.
  • Check and remove suspicious forwarding rules.
  • Make sure old accounts and shared logins are not lingering around like forgotten leftovers.

The FTC recommends practical basics like MFA, access limits, and strong account protection, and NCSC guidance similarly emphasizes sensible account controls and reducing unnecessary exposure.

Worked example: a small business email breach

Say a three-person business notices the owner’s inbox has a forwarding rule sending all mail to an unfamiliar address.

  1. The owner stops using that account and signs out other sessions.
  2. The password is changed, recovery details are checked, and any unknown forwarding rule is deleted.
  3. The inbox is reviewed for sent messages, password reset emails, and other suspicious changes.
  4. The business checks whether any client messages, invoices, or personal information may have been exposed.
  5. The issue is documented with screenshots and a timeline.
  6. If customer or financial data may be involved, the business contacts legal counsel and its insurer, then decides whether law enforcement or affected service providers should be notified.
  7. After the cause is understood, the business enables MFA, trims admin access, and reviews recovery settings so the same trick does not walk right back in.

That is the whole flow: contain it, document it, notify when needed, and fix the weak spot. Very glamorous, in the way only incident response can be.

When to get outside help

Bring in outside help if the breach may involve:

  • Customer data.
  • Employee records.
  • Payment data.
  • Regulated information.
  • Multiple accounts, systems, or devices.
  • Signs that the attacker still has access.

Legal counsel can help with notification duties and liability questions. Incident-response help can be useful if you cannot tell how the attacker got in, what they touched, or whether the compromise is still active. Law enforcement may be appropriate when fraud, extortion, or broader criminal activity is involved.

If the incident is bigger than one inbox or one laptop, do not try to solve it with vibes and heroic Googling. That usually ends with more confusion and a longer recovery.
