What Signs Show Credential Stuffing Is Targeting My Site?

Published 2026-11-07

TL;DR: Credential stuffing usually shows up as repeated failed logins across multiple accounts, bursts of attempts from unfamiliar places or devices, and then signs of takeover like strange forwarding rules or recovery changes. For small businesses, the big clues are repetition, scale, and account changes that follow stolen-password testing, not a single user typo.

Intro

If your site is being hit by credential stuffing, the signs usually look like repeated automated login attempts against real accounts, weird sign-in patterns, and account changes that suggest stolen credentials are being tested rather than a person simply typing the wrong password. CISA describes credential stuffing as automated login attempts using stolen usernames and passwords, and reused passwords make that attack much more likely to work, which is why this gets messy so quickly. CISA: What is a credential stuffing attack?

What credential stuffing looks like in practice

For a small business owner, the pattern is usually less “one frustrated user” and more “someone brought a machine gun to a lock.” You may see many login attempts across different accounts, failed sign-ins arriving in bursts, and attempts coming from places, devices, or browser setups that do not match how your staff normally works.

That matters because credential stuffing is not just random guessing. It is repeated testing of stolen login pairs at scale. So the noise tends to spread across accounts, not stay stuck on one person’s typo.

Signs in your sign-in logs and alerts

The most useful clues are often sitting in your sign-in logs and security alerts, even if you do not have an IT team babysitting them.

Look for:

  • Repeated failed logins for the same account or several accounts in a short span.

  • Attempts against accounts that should not be active anymore, like former employee accounts.

  • Alerts about impossible or unusual sign-ins, if your service provides them, especially when the location, device, or sign-in pattern does not fit normal staff behavior.

CISA and NCSC guidance both point to suspicious authentication activity and account compromise clues as things to watch for. That is the whole game here, spot the weirdness before it turns into a full-on account takeover. CISA NCSC

Signs your email account may already be affected

Business email is where credential stuffing can get extra annoying, because once someone gets in, they often do more than just peek around.

Watch for:

  • New forwarding rules you did not create.
  • Inbox changes, such as filters, labels, or folder rules that move messages out of sight.
  • Password reset or recovery setting changes you did not make.
  • Unfamiliar sent messages.
  • Missing messages that should have been there.

Those are classic takeover clues. FTC and NIST guidance both push the idea that account security is not just about the password, it is also about checking what else changed around the account and responding quickly when something looks off. FTC NIST

Worked example

A small office notices several employee accounts failing to sign in over a short period. Then one account suddenly succeeds from a location the owner does not recognize. A little later, that mailbox has a new forwarding rule, and the user receives a password reset notice they did not request.

That is a pretty clean example of credential stuffing moving from login abuse to account takeover. First the attacker sprays stolen credentials, then one reused password lands, then the mailbox gets changed so the attacker can keep access or hide activity. The problem started at login, but it does not stay there for long.

What to do right away if you see these signs

If the pattern looks real, move fast and keep it simple.

  1. Check whether the affected password was reused on any other business accounts. Reuse is the fuel here.
  2. Change passwords on the affected accounts, and on any other account that used the same password. If one password was leaked, treat the rest like they might be on borrowed time too.
  3. Turn on multi-factor authentication where it is available. That makes stolen passwords much less useful. How to set up multi-factor authentication for a small team
  4. Review forwarding rules, recovery options, and any other account settings that could let someone stay in control after the password changes.
  5. Document what changed before you restore normal access, including logins, account settings, and anything suspicious you found. That helps if you need to trace what happened later.

FTC, CISA, and NIST guidance all support this kind of response, which is basically: cut off the bad access, secure the account, and record what happened before the trail goes cold.

How to tell credential stuffing from ordinary login trouble

Ordinary login trouble is usually a one-off, someone forgot a password, mistyped it, or got locked out once. Credential stuffing looks different because it repeats, spreads, and leaves matching signs across multiple accounts.

The big clues are repetition, scale, and pattern.

If you want the broader response playbook, it also helps to keep a simple incident response plan and use it when login weirdness starts stacking up.
