How to Check Whether Your Business Email Appears in a Data Breach

Published 2026-11-17

TL;DR: Check the exact business mailbox, search a trusted breach lookup source, and read the result in context. A hit may mean the address, a reused password, or a linked account was exposed, so confirm what data was leaked, then change reused passwords, enable multi-factor authentication, and review forwarding and recovery settings.

Start with the exact email address and account name

Use the mailbox you actually want to check, not the one you think someone probably meant. Start with the exact business address, plus any aliases or role-based mailboxes like accounts@, billing@, or info@ if they can receive mail or reset links. If the address belongs to a shared inbox, note who uses it and what it connects to. That matters because the same email can sit at the center of email, password resets, and account recovery. CISA and NIST treat account protection as more than whether the inbox still opens, so begin with the real identifier, not a guess.

Check a trusted breach lookup source

Use a government or standards-based breach check service or advisory first, then search the exact email address in known breach records. If the source shows a hit, note the service or incident name and what data was exposed, such as passwords, email addresses, or other account details. CISA, FTC, NIST, and NCSC all steer people toward basic account security checks, password changes, and monitoring when credentials may have been exposed, which is why a breach match should be treated as a real warning, not a trivia result.

Read the result carefully

A breach lookup result needs context, because not every hit means the mailbox itself was taken over. Separate these cases:

  • A true account match, where the email and some account data were exposed.
  • A password-only exposure, where the email may not have been the main problem but the password was.
  • A marketing list leak, where the address was exposed but the risk is mostly phishing and spam.
  • An old personal account with the same name, which can look alarming and still be irrelevant to the business.

This is where people get tripped up by a false sense of relevance. A hit matters only if you know what data was in the record and whether that data overlaps with business access.

Worked example: what the result means for a small business mailbox

Say accounts@business.example appears in a breach record tied to an old vendor account. That does not automatically mean the current mailbox is open to an attacker, but it does mean someone may know an email and password combination that was once used for business work.

The owner should first check whether that password was reused anywhere else, especially for the mailbox itself, admin logins, customer portals, or cloud tools. NIST and the FTC both push strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication, because reused passwords are where one old leak turns into a new problem.

Then check the mailbox for signs that someone else has been there. Look for suspicious sign-in alerts, forwarding changes, recovery email or phone changes, and password reset messages you did not request. If the old vendor password still works anywhere, assume the same secret may be exposed in other places too. That is the risk with password reuse. It is a small shortcut with a loud echo.

Confirm whether the breach affects more than the inbox

A business email can matter even if the inbox itself still opens normally. Check whether the exposed address is also used for admin logins, password recovery, customer portals, billing systems, or cloud tools. If it is, the breach may affect access beyond email.

This is why an email breach check is really an identity check. The inbox is one door. The same address might also be the key used to reset other doors.

What to do if the email shows up

If the email appears in a breach record, change any reused passwords right away and make the new ones unique. Turn on multi-factor authentication where it is available. Review mailbox forwarding and recovery settings, because attackers often prefer a quiet redirect to a noisy break-in. Sign out other sessions if the service allows it, then review recent sign-in history for anything unusual.

CISA, FTC, NIST, and NCSC guidance all point in the same practical direction here: protect the account, replace exposed credentials, and monitor for follow-on abuse. If the breach may involve customer data or money movement, preserve notes about what you found so you can report or investigate further with a clear timeline.

If you want a plain-language follow-up on password reuse, what to do if your password has been leaked fits here because many breach matches turn out to be password problems first.

What to do if the email does not show up

Treat a clean result as incomplete, not as proof that everything is fine. A breach lookup only covers records the source knows about, and business mailboxes can still face phishing, odd sign-in attempts, or stolen-password testing even if they never appear in a database hit.

Keep an eye out for unusual logins, unexpected sign-in prompts, password reset emails you did not request, and mailbox settings that changed behind your back. If the account or a reused password has been under attack, the absence of a lookup hit does not mean the attackers stopped trying.

Keep a short breach-check record

Write down the email checked, the source used, the date, the result, and the next action. Keep it short and boring. Boring records are useful. They save time later when someone asks whether the business already checked a mailbox or changed a password after a breach alert.

A simple note is enough:

  • Email checked
  • Lookup source
  • Date checked
  • Result or breach name
  • Next step taken

That gives the business a repeatable trail without making anyone reconstruct the whole thing from memory and caffeine.
