What are the downsides of two-factor authentication?

Published 2026-11-16

TL;DR: Two-factor authentication lowers risk, but it can also slow logins, disrupt work when a device is missing, and still fail if attackers hit the second factor or the recovery process. For small businesses, the key is to use 2FA with solid backup access, controlled resets, and other basic account protections.

The main downsides of two-factor authentication

Two-factor authentication, or 2FA, is still a strong security move, but it is not free. It adds login friction, can create recovery headaches when the second factor is unavailable, and can still fail if an attacker reaches the second factor or the recovery process. That tradeoff is reflected in official guidance from NIST, CISA, and the FTC, which treat authentication as something to design carefully, not just switch on and forget.

It adds an extra step for every sign-in

The most obvious downside is the one people feel right away. Every login takes longer, every sign-in asks for one more action, and staff may start looking for shortcuts if the process feels annoying. NIST and NCSC both treat authentication as a balance between security and usability.

For a small business, that friction can show up in awkward places. Someone on the road may delay checking email because they do not want to reach for a phone or authenticator app. Another person may choose the weakest available workaround because they are in a hurry. None of that makes 2FA bad, but it does mean extra steps change how people behave.

It can disrupt work when the second factor is unavailable

2FA works best when the second factor is actually there. If a phone is lost, dead, out of signal, replaced, or simply left on the kitchen counter, sign-in can stop cold. That is annoying for one person and disruptive for a small team, especially if the account in question is business email, shared documents, or a key admin account.

CISA and NIST both emphasize planning for account recovery and backup methods before you depend on MFA in production. That is the part people skip when everything is working, then regret later when the old phone is gone and the replacement has not been enrolled yet. Recovery can become a business interruption if no backup access was arranged in advance.

Recovery and backup access can become a weak point

The flip side of backup access is that backup access has to be controlled. If reset rules are loose, backup codes are stored badly, or the help desk has too much power with too little checking, an attacker may use the recovery path instead of the normal login path. That is why it needs guardrails.

NIST and CISA both push secure account recovery and administration for a reason. Poorly managed recovery can lock out employees, or worse, give an intruder access to business email. For a small business, that usually means the business itself should control reset methods, keep backup codes somewhere sensible, and avoid letting a casual workflow turn into “sure, who are you again?”

Not all 2FA methods protect equally well

2FA is a category, not a guarantee. Some methods are more resistant to phishing and interception than others, and some are easier for an attacker to trick or reuse. CISA and NIST both distinguish stronger and weaker approaches, which matters because “two factors” sounds solid right up until someone is duped into approving the wrong prompt or entering a code into a fake page.

So yes, 2FA helps against stolen passwords, but it does not magically stop credential theft, token theft, or social engineering if the attacker reaches the second factor. That is the part that trips people up. The label says authentication, but the real question is how the authentication works.

It can create false confidence

There is a classic business move where someone says, “We turned on 2FA, done and dusted,” and then stops looking at everything else. That is where false confidence sneaks in. FTC and CISA guidance both treat MFA as one part of a broader security setup, not a replacement for unique passwords, access limits, session review, and sensible recovery settings.

This matters most for business email, because email is often the control room for password resets, approvals, and account recovery. If email is protected by 2FA but the account has weak recovery rules, old sessions still active, or sloppy sharing habits, the whole setup can still wobble. Security likes layers. One lock on one door is not a strategy.

Worked example: when 2FA slows a small business down

Say a small business owner uses the same phone for email sign-in and customer messages. The phone is lost during a weekend trip. Monday morning arrives, and staff cannot get into business email because the owner was the only person with the second factor.

Now the upside shows up. A thief with the old password still cannot just stroll in. But the downside shows up too, because the business has to use its backup access and recovery rules to get back into the account. If those rules were never set up properly, the company spends the morning doing identity checks, hunting for codes, and trying not to lock itself out further. The protection is real, and so is the interruption.

If you are setting this up for the first time, How to Set Up Multi-Factor Authentication for a Small Team is useful because it focuses on rollout and recovery planning, not just the setup step.

How to reduce the downsides without removing 2FA

Start with the strongest method you can support, especially one that is harder to phish, and use weaker methods only if you truly have to. CISA and NIST both support choosing better authentication methods when possible.

Set up backup access before rollout, not after someone loses a phone. CISA and NIST both stress recovery planning and backup methods, because the best time to discover your fallback is not when nobody can log in.

Keep recovery controls under business control. That means written rules for resets, limited admin rights, and care with backup codes and help-desk steps. NIST and CISA guidance on secure administration and recovery exists for a reason.

Document what to do if a device is lost or replaced. The FTC’s small-business guidance and CISA’s practical security advice both point toward planning ahead, keeping access limited, and making sure the business can still operate if one device disappears. If you need a broader checklist for email and account protection, What should a small business do after a password leak? helps connect 2FA with the rest of the recovery steps.

When 2FA is still worth using

Even with the annoyances, 2FA is usually still worth it for business email and other important accounts. Password-only sign-in is too easy to abuse. The trick is to treat 2FA as part of the setup, not the whole setup, and to pair it with good recovery controls and other access safeguards.

That is the real tradeoff for small businesses. 2FA can slow people down and cause recovery pain, but it also blocks a lot of low-effort account takeovers. Used well, it is still a net win.
